Turning a conjecture into four theorems

A 2024 paper found that optimization algorithms searching for spherical designs sometimes get stuck. The authors conjectured that the optimization landscape contains non-global local minima, but gave no proof. In two hours, Object Run found four such local minima and produced a rigorous mathematical proof for each.

Background

A spherical design is a finite set of points on the sphere whose average over any low-degree polynomial equals that polynomial's average over the whole sphere. Such point sets are used in numerical integration, experimental design and coding theory. arXiv:2405.19353 casts the search for a spherical design as an optimization problem. For 24 unit vectors in six dimensions, the goal is to minimize

F(V) = Σi,j ⟨vi, vj⟩4 − 36

When F reaches 0, the vectors form the design the paper is looking for. In Example 5.2 the paper observes that gradient-type methods sometimes stop where F is clearly above 0, and conjectures from this that the landscape has non-global local minima. But that is only a conjecture: a stopping point could also be a saddle point, or simply slow convergence. Turning the conjecture into a theorem needs a rigorous proof.

Setup

The task was to find at least one non-global local minimum that can be rigorously proven. The model wrote multi-start search, high-precision refinement and perturbation-check programs, and designed a procedure that leaves a saddle point along a direction of negative curvature and keeps descending. All 29,359 configurations saved during the search were recomputed by an independent verifier (Riemannian gradient, Hessian spectrum and perturbation response); none showed file corruption or computational error.

Results

Four classes of configurations passed every check, with energies of about 0.1237, 0.1373, 0.1768 and 0.2968. The four energy intervals are pairwise disjoint, so no two of them are related by a global rotation, a reordering of the points or a change of signs. After rigorous normalization, the paper's target design has an energy bounded above by 5.9×10−12, below all four minima, so none of the four is a global optimum.

Proof

For each minimum, we placed the 183-dimensional KKT system in a box of dyadic rationals with radius 2−180 and used outward-rounded interval arithmetic to verify two things: the box contains a stationary point that satisfies every unit-length constraint, and, after removing the 15 directions corresponding to global O(6) rotations, the Hessian is strictly positive definite on the remaining 105 tangent directions. Together these show that the point in the box is a strict local minimum modulo rotation.

The proofs were a joint effort between the model and us. We provided a reproduction of the paper's results, the verifier and the interval proof program for the first minimum. The model selected the second class of candidates and rewrote the proof program in a form based on an augmented system and Cholesky congruence transforms. The third class was found by one random seed of an existing solver, which the model recognized and refined; the fourth came from the saddle-descent procedure the model designed.

Notes

The proofs are computer-assisted, based on Arb interval arithmetic. This experiment proves the existence of four local minima; it does not classify all local minima. The run made 265 model requests, cost US$1.66 in API spend and took 2 hours 2 minutes.
